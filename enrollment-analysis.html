Visual Arts Enrollment — Trend Analysis

Texas Woman’s University · Fall 2021 — Fall 2026 · 11 terms · source: department SQL enrollment reports

The one-sentence version

Total undergraduate headcount is down 19% from its Fall 2024 peak, but that number is misleading: the department lost 90 seniors and gained 50 freshmen and sophomores over the same span. This is not a recruiting failure — it is a pipeline inversion, and it moves the department’s central risk from recruitment to retention.

1. What the headcount number hides

Undergraduate majors peaked at 206 in Fall 2024 and sit at 167 today. Read alone, that is a 19% decline. Read by class standing, it is something else entirely.

What this almost certainly means

A department cannot hold 109 seniors against 10 freshmen through internal progression — there is no cohort flow that produces that shape. The historical majors were overwhelmingly late declarers and high-hour transfers: students who arrived at the major already at or near 90 credit hours. That model supplied a large, cheap, fast-graduating major population.

That pipeline has largely stopped, and first-time-in-college recruitment has partly replaced it. The two models have completely different economics — which is the whole point of what follows.

2. Why this changes what you have to be good at

  • A student who declares at 90 hours is 2–3 semesters from a diploma. Retention risk is minimal, advising load is light, and the department books a major that was recruited, in effect, by someone else.
  • A freshman is 8 semesters from a diploma. Every one of those semesters is an opportunity to lose them, and the losses compound. Holding 52 freshmen to graduation requires sustained retention performance the department has no track record in, because it has never had to do it at this scale.
  • The work shifts from the top of the major to the bottom. In Fall 2021 you served 16 lower-division majors and 109 seniors. Today it is 90 and 42. That is a 5.6× increase in demand for foundations and intro sequences against a 60% collapse in demand for capstone and advanced studio.
The number that decides whether this story is good or bad

Ninety seniors left the rolls. If they graduated, this is a healthy generational turnover and the department is mid-transition. If they attrited, it is a slow-motion emergency. The enrollment data cannot tell these apart — it counts who is enrolled, never why someone stopped being enrolled.

Pull degrees-conferred by term for the same period and lay it against the senior line. Nothing else in this analysis matters as much, and it is a single report request.

3. Where the tracks actually stand

The aggregate hides real divergence between programs. Two are rebuilding from the bottom; one is running out of runway.

Why the share chart is misleading on its own

All three tracks moved almost identically: Art Education +35 points, Graphic Design +34, Studio Art +33. Art Education only looks like the standout because it dipped in Spring 2026 and then jumped, making its final segment the steepest line on the chart.

And the rise is only half about recruiting. Holding each side fixed to isolate the effect, Art Education’s share gained +13 points from lower-division growth and +17 points from seniors leaving. A shrinking denominator does most of the work. Absolute intake is the honest signal.

Read this before using the table above

In the Fall 2026 student-level report, 33 of 159 BFA records (21%) carry a class standing that disagrees with the student’s own completed credit hours — and every single one errs the same way, reporting a level below what the hours indicate. Nine students listed as juniors have 90+ hours; thirteen listed as freshmen have 30+.

That one-directional pattern is the signature of a field stamped at an earlier census point and never refreshed, so it misses hours earned in the intervening spring and summer. It is not random error, and it is almost certainly present in every prior term of this dataset too.

What survives it: the inversion, because a consistent lag shifts every term equally and leaves the trend intact. What does not: any single-term point estimate. On reported standing Graphic Design’s intake looks like it holds its size; on credit hours it falls 31 short — a bigger absolute gap than Art Education’s. I would not act on the steady-state numbers until the registrar confirms how ACAD_CLASS is derived.

Median hours for a BFA senior is 117. Just under half the senior population has already earned enough credit for the degree and has not graduated. By track: Art Education 6 of 11 seniors past 120 hours, Graphic Design 9 of 18, Studio Art 5 of 13.

Students at 130, 148, 174, 204 hours are not short on credits — they are short on specific credits. That is the signature of course access, not student effort: a required course offered once every two years, a prerequisite chain that only unlocks in one direction, a capstone with a hard seat cap. Each such student pays for extra semesters and each one sits in your headcount looking like an enrolled major rather than a stalled one.

Why this matters more than it looks

This is the one finding in the analysis that the scheduler can act on directly. Cross the 20 over-credit students against the rotation: if several are waiting on the same course, that is a fixable scheduling decision worth roughly a semester of tuition each and a measurable bump in your completion rate.

It also partly answers the graduation-versus-attrition question. A department with visible completion friction at the top is a department where some portion of that 90-senior decline was students timing out rather than walking. Degrees-conferred still settles it.

Graphic Design — the engine, and the exposure

At 67 majors it is still 40% of the undergraduate department, and its 35 freshmen and sophomores are the largest lower-division block anywhere in the unit. It also fell hardest in raw terms (90–67) because it had the most seniors to lose (64–18). The read: healthy intake, backlog exhausted. Its Fall 2026 shape is the closest thing the department has to a normal, self-sustaining program pyramid.

Studio Art — sharpest turn toward freshmen

48 majors, but 21 of them are freshmen — 44% of the track, the largest freshman share in the department, and an intake that would sustain a program of 84 if it all held. Studio Art has swung hardest into the FTIC model.

That is genuinely promising and genuinely fragile. Its shape is the lumpiest in the department — 21 freshmen behind just 7 sophomores and 7 juniors — so almost all of its future rides on one cohort. A track that is nearly half first-years is one bad sophomore year from a visible collapse, and it will not show up in headcount for two more years. Where Art Education’s risk is structural (too small), Studio Art’s whole future rides on a single cohort: the upside is the largest in the department and so is the variance.

Art Education — smallest, and rebuilding toward a smaller floor

Down from 46 to 30, with 11 seniors left and 15 students in the lower division. Art Ed was the most senior-dependent track in the department (90% seniors in 2021), so it lost the most when the late-declarer feed stopped.

It is not, however, failing to rebuild. Its 6 freshmen are a track record — the previous high across all eleven terms was 4 — and its lower-division share rose as much as any other track. Its Fall 2026 shape (6 / 9 / 4 / 11) is reasonably even for a 30-student program.

The concern is narrower and more specific than “it isn’t recovering.” Art Ed is rebuilding toward an equilibrium below where it currently sits. Six freshmen a year sustains roughly 24 majors; the track has 30 today. Graphic Design’s 16 freshmen sustain about 64 against a current 67, and Studio’s 21 sustain about 84 against a current 48. Art Education is the only track whose intake does not support its present size — and it is the smallest track, in the category most likely to carry external certification and viability thresholds. That combination, not a failure to recruit, is what puts a clock on it.

Undeclared BFA — new, and worth watching

Sat at 1–5 students for five years, then jumped to 14 in Fall 2026 with 9 freshmen. Students are entering the BFA without choosing a track. Nationally, undeclared students are the highest-attrition population in any major, so this is simultaneously the department’s best conversion opportunity and its most likely leak.

4. The minors are the quietest good news here

Minor enrollment grew from 3 students to 46 across the same five years — a 15× increase, rising in every single term, with no reversal anywhere in the series.

  • Scientific Illustration went 0–15 and is now the department’s single largest minor. A curve that clean usually means a specific feeder relationship — a biology, pre-health, or health-sciences requirement or advising channel. Whatever that channel is, it is worth naming, formalizing, and replicating.
  • Painting & Drawing (0–9) and Art (1–6) show real non-major demand for core studio coursework.
  • Sculpture has had zero minors in all eleven terms. 3D Intermedia has had one. Ceramics has never exceeded two. The 3D areas are not reaching non-majors at all, while 2D and digital areas are. This is a curricular access question — usually intro-level 3D courses that are prerequisite-locked, badly timed, materials-fee heavy, or simply not visible in the catalog to a non-major.
  • 46 minors are, in section-demand terms, a meaningful fraction of a 167-student major population, and they are invisible in every headline number the university looks at.

5. Graduate and other programs

  • MFA is stable — 14 now, 14 at the start, range 9–17, no trend. In a period when the undergraduate population turned over completely, that steadiness is a real asset and the strongest thing in the dataset to defend.
  • The MA is effectively dormant — 0–2 students for five consecutive years, currently 1. It is a program that exists on paper. Either it gets a deliberate relaunch with a defined audience, or it gets consolidated before someone upstream notices it and makes the decision for you.
  • The BA grew 0–12 and has fallen back to 7. Two years is not enough to call a trend, but it is enough to justify asking what drove the run-up and whether it was repeatable.
  • Post-baccalaureate is down to 1 from a steady 2–4.

6. What I would focus on

Ordered by how much the decision depends on getting it right, not by effort.

1
Confirm the seniors graduated
Degrees-conferred by term, laid against the senior line. This single report determines whether the last two years were a healthy turnover or a retention failure — and therefore whether the rest of this list is a growth plan or a rescue plan. Do this first.
2
Reframe the narrative before someone else frames it for you
“206 → 167” reads as a failing department to anyone who sees only that. “We replaced a transfer-fed senior population with the largest freshman class in department history” is the same data and an entirely different conversation. Lead with the class-standing chart. The window to control this framing closes the moment it lands in a provost’s dashboard.
3
Build a sophomore-year retention intervention
Sophomore-to-junior is the only cohort flow in the series running below 100%, and in a studio department it is the classic break point — after foundations, before the major sequence commits. With 38 sophomores and 52 freshmen behind them, this transition now governs the department’s headcount two years out. It is also the cheapest place to gain students, because they are already enrolled.
4
Re-weight the schedule toward the bottom of the major
Lower-division demand is up 5.6×; senior demand is down 60%. If the SP27–FA28 rotation still assumes the old shape, you are running under-enrolled advanced sections while capping the intro courses that now decide retention. This is the most direct link between this analysis and the scheduler — and the misallocation compounds every term it goes uncorrected.
5
Give Art Education a dedicated plan
Its intake is rising — 6 freshmen is a track record — but it is the one track whose intake does not support its current size on either measure, and it is the smallest, in the category most likely to face an external viability threshold. The risk is its floor, not its trajectory. Note that on the credit-hours basis Graphic Design’s gap is larger in absolute terms; Art Ed’s danger is that it is small enough for the gap to matter.
6
Work the minors deliberately
Find out what is driving Scientific Illustration and whether it can be replicated. Build a minor-to-major conversion touchpoint. Separately, ask why the 3D areas draw zero non-majors — that is a fixable access problem, not a demand problem, and it is the clearest untapped capacity in the department.
7
Catch the undeclared BFA cohort early
Fourteen students, nine of them freshmen, no declared track. Highest attrition risk in the building and the easiest group to help. A structured track-selection touchpoint in the first year converts a leak into six years of enrollment.

7. What this data cannot tell you

Read these before acting on anything above

Fall 2026 is a pre-semester snapshot. The source note says the report was re-run 8/5/2026 and the term has not started. Late registration — which skews toward first-years — is not in it. Fall 2026 is a floor, not a final count, and should not be compared like-for-like against completed terms.

Fall 2026 also changed source reports. Every prior term reads “Imported from SQL report”; Fall 2026 reads “Derived from Visual Arts Scheduling Data report.” If class standing is computed differently in the new report, some of the Fall 2026 shift is method, not students. The trend clearly predates the source change — it starts in Fall 2024 — but I would not trust the magnitude of the final jump until the definition is confirmed identical.

These are counts, not students. Cohort flows above 100% mean new students entered at that level; they mask whatever losses happened underneath. Real retention rates require student-level term-to-term persistence data, which this dataset does not contain. Every retention statement above is therefore directional, not measured.

No transfer-versus-FTIC flag. The late-declarer explanation for the historical senior bulge is the only shape that fits the numbers, but it is an inference. One report field would confirm it.
